Data Science & Causal Inference

Environmental Health Modeling & Causal Inference

1. Executive Summary

This project investigates the relationship between long-term PM2.5 air pollution exposure and asthma prevalence in California (2018). By harmonizing CDC Chronic Disease Indicators, satellite-derived PM2.5 estimates, and ACS 5-Year Demographic data at the Census Block Group level, we pursued a dual-track analysis: 1) Causal Inference to estimate the Average Treatment Effect (ATE) of high pollution on asthma, and 2) Predictive Modeling comparing Generalized Linear Models (GLM) and Random Forests (RF) to predict PM2.5 levels using demographic indicators.

2. Data Engineering & Treatment Assignment

A critical challenge in the causal analysis was defining the "treatment" (High PM2.5 exposure) from a continuous variable. The empirical PM2.5 distribution exhibited a distinct bimodal shape.

Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) Binarization

  • Fitted a 2-component Gaussian Mixture Model to the PM2.5 density.
  • Assigned each Census Block Group to "High" (Treatment=1) or "Low" (Treatment=0) clusters based on the maximum posterior probability.
  • This data-driven approach cleanly separated the distributions (Low median ≈ 28, High median ≈ 75), avoiding arbitrary manual thresholds and enabling robust propensity score estimation.

3. Causal Inference (IPW & Bootstrapping)

To estimate the causal effect of high PM2.5 on adult asthma prevalence, we utilized Inverse Propensity Weighting (IPW) to adjust for structural confounders, including race, income, poverty rates, and occupational distributions.

Findings & ATE

After IPW adjustment, the estimated Average Treatment Effect (ATE) was approximately -0.38 percentage points.

This counterintuitive negative effect highlights the complexities of ecological inference, potentially driven by unmeasured confounders (e.g., urban healthcare access) or clinical thresholds not captured by spatial clusters.

Uncertainty Quantification

To assess the stability of this estimate, we performed 1,000 Bootstrap resamples.

Mean ATE: -0.37
95% CI: [-0.45, -0.30]

The confidence interval does not cross zero, indicating a statistically distinct effect within the confines of our structural causal model.

4. Predictive Modeling: GLM vs. Random Forest

We evaluated whether demographic features could accurately predict PM2.5 concentrations, comparing a parametric approach (GLM) against a non-parametric ensemble (Random Forest). To address severe multicollinearity, redundant variables (e.g., raw Income) were dropped in favor of orthogonalized features (IncomePerCap, ChildPoverty).

The "Log + Z" GLM Architecture

We deployed a Gaussian GLM with an identity link. However, predicting raw values led to heavy heteroscedasticity. By applying a `log1p()` transformation to the target variable and standardizing (Z-scoring) the predictors, the model fit improved drastically.

AIC Drop: 57,647 (Plain) ➔ 10,558 (Log+Z)

Random Forest & Permutation Importance

An RF Regressor (n_estimators=600, max_depth=20) was trained to capture non-linear socioeconomic interactions. It achieved a 5-fold CV R-squared of 0.376.

Top Drivers (Permutation Decrease in R-squared):

  • 1. White Population Proportion (0.310)
  • 2. Production Occupation (0.166)
  • 3. Hispanic Population Proportion (0.113)

5. Conclusion & Generalization

The evaluation revealed a classic bias-variance tradeoff. The Random Forest achieved superior accuracy on same-state/same-year data (RMSE 19.97) due to its ability to model complex non-linearities. However, the GLM proved far more robust when extrapolating to out-of-sample temporal and spatial domains (e.g., Washington 2017), suffering less from distribution drift. The combination of structural causal models and rigorous predictive validation underscores the importance of spatial equity when analyzing environmental health disparities.
